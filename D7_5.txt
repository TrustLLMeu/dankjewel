Deliverable 7.5:
Protocol for a Multi-Dimensional Evaluation Metric
for Text Generation
Lars Bungum, NTNU

Summary
This document defines a protocol for online evaluation of generated text for the creation
of an evaluation metric, which will provide useful information for the user as responses from
an LLM are returned. The approaches are divided into four prongs: 1) LLM-as-judge, 2)
rule-based, 3) extraction-based, and 4) intrinsic. After results are returned from each, they
are combined and visualized. The protocol defines a modular structure, which does not require that all modules are present for all target languages to work. The methods and ideas
presented in this document are grounded in peer-reviewed research, for which relevant references are presented.

Introduction

This deliverable addresses the multi-dimensional evaluation of generated text in an online setting. We define a metric through an evaluation protocol (henceforth, The Protocol). Section 4
will elaborate on the concept of online evaluation, which can currently be understood as near
real-time assessment. Henceforth, the Protocol will refer to the broader process to create a unified Metric, which in turn will refer to the actual evaluation assessment produced once the said
process is completed.
The online aspect sets this evaluation apart from conventional dataset-driven approaches,
which assess performance on predefined datasets to test Natural Language Generation (NLG)
and Understanding (NLU) capabilities. Such task-oriented performance measures are considered out of scope. The Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) dataset (Hendrycks et al.,
2021) for testing knowledge and the SST-5 dataset (Socher et al., 2013) for sentiment classification serve as examples of such tasks.
In contrast, an online metric for generated text should provide comprehensive information
to the user while remaining neutral to the prompt’s purpose. Users are assumed to understand
their intent, whether requesting truthful or ironic text. A well-designed metric will aid the
user and save human effort in assessing the quality of generated text for arbitrary purposes. A
modular architecture ensures the metric can still be computed even when not all modules are
available for every language, thereby facilitating multilinguality.
Howcroft et al. (2020) advocated for a standardization of experimental design and terminology in a review of human evaluation efforts. Accordingly, we strive to clearly define terms
like ‘online evaluation’ and clarify the underlying assumptions of our work. Chang et al. (2023)
outlined three dimensions in LLM evaluation that create a useful frame of reference to put
the Protocol into context. In their words, ’what to evaluate’ encapsulates existing evaluation
tasks for LLMs, ’where to evaluate’ involves selecting appropriate datasets and benchmarks for
evaluation, while ’how to evaluate’ is concerned with the evaluation process given appropriate
tasks and datasets.
For the Metric, ’what to evaluate’ —the tasks— are characterizations of the generated text,
such as reliability and factual accuracy. Where to evaluate - on what basis - pertains to the
datasets for rule-based characterizations of the text and also other resources such as multilingual models for embedding-based evaluation metrics. How to evaluate - the evaluation
process itself - will address the division of labor between rule-based and LLM-as-judge approaches, extraction-based methods of evaluation, as well as model characteristics at inference
time. Moreover, it relates to the presentation and visualization of the Metric.

1.1

Underlying Assumptions

The metric defined by the Protocol rests on two major assumptions. First, the metric is designed
to evaluate arbitrary generated text, assuming users know their own intentions, which are presumed benign. An example is a user requesting semantically anomalous text, in contrast to
one requesting an essay fit for publication. Moreover, this implies that performance on solving
specific Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks is defined out of scope.
Additionally, the evaluation must be computationally efficient, delivering results in near
real-time with a maximum delay of a few seconds to ensure a responsive and interactive user
experience.

Figure 1 charts the flow of the computation of the metrics. The generated text is evaluated
by a four-pronged approach, which rests on a multilingual preparation step. The design is
modular, and the tasks are not, by definition, limited to the ones indicated in the flowchart.
The data flow begins with the generated text, created with a prompt. The generated text,
along with optional computational data from the model, is then passed to the four prongs,
which compute values that are ultimately combined and visualized. Although there is some
overlap between the method families represented by the prongs, each is distinguished by its
primary source of knowledge. LLM-based approaches fundamentally rely on embeddings or
prompt-driven generation; rule-based methods draw on knowledge encoded in rules or lexica;
knowledge-grounded approaches are based on external resources such as fact databases; and
intrinsic approaches rely on knowledge derived from the weights of the LLM’s Artificial Neural Network (ANN). After the different tasks are evaluated in the respective prongs, they are
combined and visualized, as shown in the bottom of the figure.
We will exemplify each prong and provide a table of sample evaluation dimensions and
tasks, before we discuss resources and methods for their evaluation in academic literature.
Prong 1 (LLM-as-judge): Smaller models such as DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019) can produce
results quickly. An example application of this approach is to fine-tune a lightweight model on
datasets such as the Google Jigsaw1 dataset to output continuous values of toxicity. The Per1 https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/jigsaw_toxicity_pred

Abuse Technology team as a joint research effort.
Prong 2 (Rule-based): Rule-based methods include lookup measures such as lists of toxic
words, which can compute a measure for the entire text. These approaches are distinguished by
their reliance on text-based knowledge bases and rules to process the text. As datasets and rulebased methods will be used to train the LLM-as-judge approaches in the above prong, whether
or not to apply rule-based methods directly will have to be determined by the performance on
the individual evaluation dimension. The effectiveness of keeping rule-based metrics compared
to training LLMs to perform the same task will remain an empirical research question for the
work package.
Prong 3 (Extraction-based): Extraction-based methods will extract objects of analysis such as
claims from the generated text and subsequently evaluate them with Natural Language Inference. This form of evaluation encompasses second-order evaluation of the generated text that
extracts elements like claims and subsequently verifies them. Claimify is a system driven by
LLMs from Microsoft (Metropolitansky and Larson, 2025), which is evaluated on the available
BingCheck data set (Li et al., 2024b), which both extracts and evaluates claims. Because issues with reproducibility remain (e.g., model size and training data), simpler methods using
libraries like NLTK (Bird et al., 2009), SpaCy (Honnibal and Montani, 2017), or Stanza (Qi et al.,
2020) will also be used for claim extraction by applying dependency and/or POS tagging and
extracting claims with rules. As a consequence, Claimify is only considered for benchmarking
and calibration during development.
Prong 4 (Intrinsic): Intrinsic measures aggregate values from the LLMs’ underlying computation like Perplexity and Confidence. Geng et al. (2024) surveyed confidence measures and
accounted for methods to apply logits and softmax probabilities to metrics. These require cautious interpretation but complement the other dimensions when the Metric is interpreted by the
user.

Li et al. (2024a) reviewed LLM-as-judge approaches, and cited text fluency, logical coherence,
and creativity as dimensions LLMs could grasp unlike traditional metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) or ROUGE (Lin, 2004), extending beyond task performance. Furthermore, Li
et al. argue that complex and dynamic problems like from real-world scenarios such as robustness, fairness, and interpretability must be accounted for, and see LLM-as-judge approaches as
a means to this end. Fine-tuning LLMs to output e.g., continuous toxicity scores has been done
for a long time, as reflected by the OffensEval 2020 shared task report (Zampieri et al., 2020).
In addition to fine-tuning LLMs to evaluate specific dimensions, embeddings extracted from
model such as XLM-RoBERTa (Conneau et al., 2020) or the language-agnostic LaBSE (Feng et al.,
2020) model (including derivatives of either) also belong to this family of methods for semantic
similarity tasks. Chen et al. (2023a) used embeddings to measure sentence similarity. As shown

The knowledge-grounded evaluation prong aims to hold claims and statements made in running text up to the scrutiny of external knowledge-bases through inference techniques. The
extraction of claims is crucial to this step, and solutions such as the neural OpenIE (Kolluru
et al., 2020) system, which can later be checked against resources like Wikidata (Vrandecic and
Krötzsch, 2014) or FACTBANK (Saurı́ and Pustejovsky, 2021), a corpus annotated with factuality of events. Chen et al. (2023b) published good results on the FEVER dataset with methods
incorporating syntactic information. Modality awareness measures the certainty in the claims
made. Hyland (2005) laid the foundation for forays into computational methods for hedging detection, a form of modality detection. In 2010 a shared task was held on the detection of hedges

(Farkas et al., 2010). The above-mentioned Factbank corpus can be used as a knowledge-source

indicate uncertainty about factuality. Mahany et al. (2022) conducted a more recent review of
hedging (termed speculation).

Intrinsic measures refer to calculations done on the weights and loss functions in the Artificial
Neural Networks underpinning the LLMs. Nikitin et al. (2024) argue that the calculation of
uncertainty in free-form natural language generated by LLMs is an open research question, and
go on to state that:
The unique properties of LLMs and natural language preclude the application of
established methods for uncertainty quantification.
If the uncertainty of interest lies in the semantic space, measuring uncertainty in the token space
less interesting. For a sentence like ’Johnny Cash was a linebacker in the NFL’, a model could
assign high probabilities for each token, despite the fact that the statement is false. Kuhn et al.
(2023) proposed ’semantic entropy’ to account for linguistic invariances.
Nikitin et al. proposed the concept of Kernel Language Entropy, an extension of the Semantic Entropy proposed by Farquhar et al. (2024) for detecting hallucinations in LLM output.
Both methods rely on multiple samples, which creates challenges for their adaptation in an
online evaluation setting. Fang et al. (2024) argue that perplexity (PPL) is a bad measure of
uncertainty, as it loses information by averaging token probabilities uniformly, and propose the
computationally heavier metrics LongPPl and LongCE as alternatives, of which the latter can
be computed without an external evaluator. Catak and Kuzlu (2024) also propose methods for
9

measuring uncertainty by characterizing Convex Hull areas (a geometric concept) with the use
of external resources like BERT and GPT-3.

4

Online Evaluation and Combination

This section addresses how online evaluation can be practically implemented, as well as how
subresults from the four prongs can be combined and aggregated into a unified metric.

4.1

Efficiency Requirements

To ensure the practical utility of the metric in assessing running text, the response time must
be minimized. Prong 1 methods will rely on lightweight models like DistilBERT to accelerate
embedding generation and classification, while Prong 2 uses precomputed lookup tables and
cached lexicons to speed up rule-based scoring. For Prong 3, optimized spaCy pipelines with
batch processing, GPU acceleration, and cached Wikidata queries minimize latency in claim extraction and verification. Intrinsic metrics in Prong 4, such as perplexity and entropy, are computed at inference time, with necessary data available at that time. Parallel processing across
prongs and adaptive computation based on prompt complexity (e.g., skipping factual consistency checks for simple prompts) could further reduce latency. Specifying an exact time limit
is premature due to the modular approach’s optional components, but ensuring the metric’s
computation time does not exceed text generation is a reasonable goal.

4.2

Comparability

The TrustLLM project will develop LLMs for the following languages: Danish, Dutch, English,
Faroese, German, Norwegian (Bokmål and Nynorsk), and Swedish. The metric can produce
values for text from any model, but it will not be directly comparable across languages, as it is
unrealistic to expect all modules to be available for every language.

4.3

Visualization

The metric will combine scores for each prong in a weighted average, presenting intrinsic measures (Prong 4) as bar charts or color codes alongside it. A radar plot combining dimensions
such as readability, semantic coherence, and toxicity will accompany the values. Figure 2 shows
a sample radar plot with mockup data, visualizing scores for four dimensions from the first
three prongs and the confidence of Prong 4 as a color gradient. Since generating such plots is
inexpensive, a default set of elements could be offered, with options for users to include others.

4.4

Score Aggregation

necessary to acquire prong scores. Combination of these scores could be done using a weighted
sum, which lets the user prioritize dimensions. Let S1 , S2 , S3 , S4 represent the normalized
scores (0–1) for Semantic Coherence (Prong 1), Readability (Prong 2), Factual Accuracy (Prong
3), and Confidence (Prong 4), respectively. The combined score M can then be computed as:

5

Multilinguality

Multilinguality is key to the Metric defined by the Protocol. Given that the target languages
range from those with limited resources (e.g., Faroese) to widely spoken ones (e.g., English),
a modular design is essential. Acquiring language-specific resources to test each defined task
will pose a challenge. As a result, the metric must function even when not all subtasks can be
tested across all languages. Code-switched text, in particular, poses a challenge for rule-based
approaches, which will have to be compared to using broad-coverage multilingual models in
the LLM-as-judge prong.
Wikidata’s structured data supports factual consistency checks across languages, as its QIDs
and properties are language-independent, enabling verification for languages such as Norwegian, Faroese, and others (Vrandecic and Krötzsch, 2014). Furthermore, the intrinsic metrics
from Prong 4 are also language-independent.
Enevoldsen et al. (2025) published a large framework for evaluating the merits of embeddings in multilingual settings, a useful resource when considering models providing embeddings, e.g., for purposes of measuring semantic similarity.

6

Conclusion

This protocol defines the creation of a metric for the assessment of arbitrary text produced by an
LLM. The Metric is modular (functioning even if not all modules are present), online (producing output quickly), and multilingual (applicable to all target languages, though not directly
comparable across them). A specification of evaluation dimensions and tasks is divided into
four prongs, representing different sources of knowledge. For each of them, references in the

11

academic literature are presented to justify the feasibility of the approaches.
The Metric will rely on openly available resources when fine-tuning lightweight LLMs for
fast inference, or using knowledge-bases for rule-based evaluation or fact extraction. Intrinsic
data can be provided from the model at inference time to further inform the metric when it is
presented.
There can be no ground truth for the online evaluation of arbitrary running text coming
out of LLMs, as only the user knows what the prompt is, or what the desired outcome was.
Hence, it is assumed that the user will be informed by metrics such as confidence and factuality,
depending on whether high or low values are desired for these dimensions.
Since a modular design has been defined, neither an exhaustive list of the tasks that are
required to be included nor the exact combination weights of the tasks are necessary to define
at this point. That said, many interesting evaluation dimensions have been indicated, as well
as tasks to test them and resources to do this with. How many tasks that can be shifted from
rule-based to LLM-as-judge approaches remains an empirical question.
In conclusion, this document has presented a protocol that achieves the objectives outlined
above, based on the assumptions that the user understands their prompting intentions and that
the metric, designed to aid in interpreting the response, is delivered within seconds. Given
the plethora of evaluation metrics and datasets, a modular, multi-dimensional metric, akin to
dashboard instruments aiding a driver, aligns with the caution required when interpreting individual metrics.
